# Integers – Common Misconceptions

## Purpose

This resource helps teachers identify and address common misconceptions students may have when learning integers.

The focus is on understanding the meaning of positive and negative numbers rather than memorising rules.

---

## Misconception 1: A Number With a Larger Digit Is Always Greater

### Example

A student may think:

**-8 > -3**

because 8 is greater than 3.

### Why This Happens

The student may compare the absolute values without considering the position of negative numbers on the number line.

### Diagnostic Question

Which is greater?

**-8 or -3?**

Ask:

**"How did you decide?"**

### Conceptual Explanation

On a number line, numbers farther to the right are greater.

Therefore:

**-3 > -8**

### Remedial Activity

Draw a horizontal number line and mark:

**-10, -8, -5, -3, 0, 3**

Ask students to identify which number is farther to the right.

---

## Misconception 2: Adding a Negative Number Always Makes the Answer Smaller Without Understanding Why

### Example

A student may know:

**5 + (-3) = 2**

but may not understand what the negative number represents.

### Diagnostic Question

What does:

**5 + (-3)**

mean on a number line?

### Conceptual Explanation

Starting at 5 and adding -3 means moving 3 units to the left.

Therefore:

**5 + (-3) = 2**

### Remedial Activity

Use a number line.

1. Start at 5.
2. Adding +1 means move one step right.
3. Adding -1 means move one step left.
4. Model several examples.

---

## Misconception 3: Two Negative Signs Always Make a Negative Number

### Example

A student may think:

**(-3) × (-2) = -6**

because both numbers are negative.

### Why This Happens

Students may memorise incomplete sign rules without understanding the mathematical structure behind multiplication.

### Diagnostic Question

Ask:

**What do you think (-3) × (-2) means?**

Ask the student to explain the sign of the answer.

### Remedial Strategy

Use patterns:

**3 × (-2) = -6**

**2 × (-2) = -4**

**1 × (-2) = -2**

**0 × (-2) = 0**

Continue the pattern:

**(-1) × (-2) = 2**

**(-2) × (-2) = 4**

This helps students observe why the sign changes.

---

## Misconception 4: Subtraction Always Means Moving Left

### Example

A student may think:

**5 - (-3)**

means moving 3 units left from 5.

### Diagnostic Question

Represent:

**5 - (-3)**

on a number line.

### Conceptual Explanation

Subtracting a negative is equivalent to adding the corresponding positive number:

**5 - (-3) = 5 + 3 = 8**

On a number line, starting at 5 and subtracting -3 results in moving 3 units to the right.

### Remedial Activity

Use movement on a number line and physical counters representing positive and negative quantities.

---

## Misconception 5: Zero Is Neither Positive Nor Negative, So It Has No Numerical Meaning

### Diagnostic Question

Where is zero located on a number line?

Ask:

**Is zero closer to positive numbers, negative numbers, or neither?**

### Conceptual Explanation

Zero is the reference point separating positive and negative numbers.

It is:

- Neither positive nor negative
- Greater than every negative integer
- Less than every positive integer

### Remedial Activity

Use a number line and real-life contexts such as:

- Temperature
- Elevation
- Bank balance
- Building floors

# Teacher Questioning Strategies

Instead of immediately correcting an incorrect answer, ask:

- "Can you show this on a number line?"
- "Where would this number be located?"
- "Which number is farther to the right?"
- "What does the negative sign mean here?"
- "Can you explain your thinking?"
- "Can you represent this using a real-life situation?"
- "Can you find another example?"

These questions help reveal the student's reasoning.

---

# Real-Life Connections

Integers can be connected to familiar situations:

| Situation | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Above 0°C | Below 0°C |
| Bank balance | Money available | Amount owed |
| Elevation | Above sea level | Below sea level |
| Building floors | Floors above ground | Basement floors |
| Movement | Forward/right | Backward/left |

Real-life contexts can help students connect abstract integer operations to meaningful situations.

---

# Teacher Observation

When working with a student, record:

1. The question asked
2. The student's answer
3. The student's explanation
4. The misconception observed
5. The strategy used
6. The student's response after remediation

The explanation is often more informative than the final answer.

---

# Reassessment

After remediation, provide a similar but different problem.

For example:

Initial problem:

**-8 or -3 — which is greater?**

Follow-up problem:

**-6 or -2 — which is greater?**

Ask the student to explain the reasoning again.

The aim is to determine whether the student can apply the concept independently.
